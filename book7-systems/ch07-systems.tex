\chapter{Hazards: Forwarding, Stalls, Branches}
\label{ch:hazards07}
\emergencystretch=3em

Ch06 ended with a fast liar: the naive pipeline retired one
instruction per cycle while handing stale registers to dependent
code --- \texttt{a1=3} where 8 belonged. This chapter pays the bill
in hardware. Three fixes, each small enough to draw on one figure:
\emph{forwarding} muxes that route results backward before
writeback; a \emph{load-use stall} that freezes fetch for the one
case forwarding cannot reach; and \emph{predict-not-taken plus
flush} for branches. Then the same testbench ch06 failed: now
\texttt{a0=5, a1=8, a2=41, a3=42, dmem[1]=42}, at a measured cost
of exactly one pipeline bubble. Every number in this chapter comes
from the real core.

\begin{wins}[What you will be able to do after this chapter]
You will be able to look at a short instruction sequence and say, before
running anything, how many extra cycles a pipelined RV32I core will
spend on it: zero for ALU-to-ALU dependencies, one for a load followed
by its user, two for every taken branch. You will be able to check
that prediction against the real core with the cycle formula
$F = I + \text{stalls} + 2\cdot\text{taken}$, and to tell whether a
test is any good by breaking the hardware on purpose and seeing it
fail.
\end{wins}

\begin{rulebox}[Before you start]
From ch06: a five-stage pipeline (IF, ID, EX, MEM, WB) overlaps five
instructions, one per stage, with registers between stages; a later
instruction that reads a register an earlier one is still computing
gets a stale value, and a read is safe only if the reader is at least
three instructions behind the writer in our design. From ch05: the ALU
computes from two operands, the program counter (\texttt{pc}) names the
next instruction, a \texttt{beq}/\texttt{bne} compares by subtracting
and testing for zero. From Book 5 ch09 (combinational circuits): a
\emph{multiplexer} picks one of several inputs by a select signal, and
comparators (\texttt{==}) are a few gates. From Book 5 ch12: a
testbench drives the clock and checks results. That is all.
\end{rulebox}

\section{The idea from zero: shortcuts, waiting, and guessing}
\label{sec:zero07}

Return to the assembly line. Suppose station 3 needs a part that
station 4 is just finishing. There are three honest things the
foreman can do. (1) \emph{Take a shortcut}: hand the part sideways from
station 4 to station 3 the moment it exists, instead of sending it
down to the warehouse (the register file) and back. That is
\emph{forwarding}. (2) \emph{Wait}: if the part genuinely does not exist
yet, stop station 3 for one tick and let a blank item pass through. That
is a \emph{stall} (the blank is a \emph{bubble}). (3) \emph{Guess}: when
the next job depends on a decision not yet made (a branch), start the
most likely one and be ready to throw it away. That is \emph{prediction}
with a \emph{flush}.

\begin{principle}[Think like this: make the common case free, the rare case correct]
Hardware designers do not try to avoid every delay. They ask which
situations are common and make those cost nothing (ALU-to-ALU
dependencies: forwarding), and make sure the rare, expensive
situations are at least \emph{correct} (a load followed at once by its
user: a one-cycle stall). Correctness is never negotiable; speed is
what you buy with whatever logic you can afford.
\end{principle}

\section{Forwarding: don't wait for writeback}
\label{sec:fwd07}
\index{forwarding}\index{bypass}

\begin{example}[the value is already there]\label{ex:fwd07}
When \texttt{addi a0,zero,5} is in MEM, its result sits in the
EX/MEM register; when it's in WB, in MEM/WB. The consumer's ALU
input just needs a mux pointed at those registers instead of only
the regfile. Two comparisons per operand --- \texttt{exmem\_rd ==
idex\_rs1? memwb\_rd == idex\_rs1?} --- newest wins.
\end{example}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{figures/ch07/fig_fwdpath}
\caption[Forwarding paths]{Two muxes on the ALU inputs, newest
first: EX/MEM, then MEM/WB, then the register file.}
\label{fig:fwd07}
\end{figure}

\begin{checkpoint}[why newest first]\label{cp:newest07}
If \texttt{a0} is written by two back-to-back instructions, both
EX/MEM and MEM/WB may match. The newer writer must win --- ordering
the mux checks EX/MEM first is the whole subtlety.
\end{checkpoint}

\begin{example}[forwarded, measured]\label{ex:fwdmeas07}
Same pair that broke ch06: \texttt{addi a0,zero,5; addi a1,a0,3}.
Real tb output:
\begin{lstlisting}
a0=5 a1=8 -- forwarding fixed ch06's 3
dmem[1]=42 -- dependent store forwarded
measured: 1 stalls, 6 WB-writes in 40 cycles
\end{lstlisting}
\texttt{a1=8} at full speed --- zero bubbles for ALU hazards. The
\texttt{sw} got its store data forwarded to the MEM stage too.
\end{example}

\section{Load-use: the one stall you cannot avoid}
\label{sec:loaduse07}
\index{load-use hazard}\index{stall}

\begin{example}[memory is one cycle too slow]\label{ex:loaduse07}
\texttt{lw a2,0(x); addi a3,a2,1}: in real hardware a load's data
come out of the memory at the \emph{end} of the MEM cycle, one stage
\emph{after} the consumer's ALU needs them at the start of its EX. No
wire can run backwards in time. (One could build a path from the
memory output straight into the next ALU in the same cycle, but then
the clock period must cover memory \emph{plus} ALU in series, wiping
out the point of pipelining.) The honest fix: the hazard unit detects
that the EX instruction is a load (\texttt{idex\_memread}) whose
destination matches the ID instruction's \texttt{rs1} or \texttt{rs2},
freezes \texttt{pc} and \texttt{ifid}, and bubbles EX once; the load
data are then forwarded from MEM/WB. Measured cost: \textbf{exactly 1
stall} --- the tb counts it.
\end{example}

\centering

the load's MEM data now forwards into its EX.}

\begin{checkpoint}[why can't we forward the load?]\label{cp:load07}
Forwarding moves data backward through stages already computed. The
load's MEM stage hasn't produced the value when the consumer's EX
runs --- it exists only a cycle later, so the consumer must wait
one cycle; then MEM/WB forwarding covers it.
\end{checkpoint}

\begin{example}[take the stall away and watch it break]\label{ex:nostall07}
How do you know the stall is needed rather than just believing the
argument? Remove it. The test script builds a copy of the core in which
the stall condition is forced to 0 and runs the sum program (which has
\texttt{lw a0,0(zero); addi a1,a0,1}). The correct final state has
\texttt{a1}=56 and memory word 1 equal to 56. The mutant core, run in
the Linux x86\_64 container (Icarus 12), prints:
\begin{lstlisting}
CORE=fwd-pipe F=55 stalls=0 taken=9 lastret=-1
x5=0000000b
x6=00000037
x7=0000000b
x10=00000037
x11=00000001
m0=00000037
m1=00000001
\end{lstlisting}
One cycle faster (55 instead of 56), and \texttt{a1}=1: the \texttt{addi}
added 1 to the load's \emph{address} (0), because that is the only
thing the EX/MEM register holds for a load. The reference model says
56. Note a subtlety that we found along the way: an earlier version of
this core forwarded the memory output combinationally into the next
ALU, which made the stall unnecessary \emph{in simulation}, where
memory answers instantly. That path is removed here so the model
matches real memory timing.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[forwarding priority, broken on purpose]\label{ex:prio07}
Two back-to-back writes of \texttt{a0} followed by a reader
(\texttt{ch07\_prio.S}: \texttt{li a0,1; addi a0,a0,1; add a1,a0,zero}).
When the \texttt{add} is in EX, EX/MEM holds the newer \texttt{a0}
(2) and MEM/WB the older one (1). Correct: \texttt{a0}=2, \texttt{a1}=2.
The mutant that tests MEM/WB \emph{first} gives
\begin{lstlisting}
CORE=fwd-pipe F=3 stalls=0 taken=0 lastret=-1
x10=00000002
x11=00000001
\end{lstlisting}
\texttt{a1}=1: the reader saw the stale writer. The test would pass for
any program that never has two writers in flight, which is why this
program exists.
\end{example}

\section{Branches: predict, verify, flush}
\label{sec:branch07}
\index{control hazard}\index{branch prediction}\index{flush}

\begin{example}[the cheapest predictor]\label{ex:pred07}
Always fetch \texttt{pc+4} (predict not-taken). If EX proves a
branch taken, squash the two wrong-path slots --- NOP into IF/ID,
bubble into ID/EX, redirect pc to the computed target. Two lost
slots per misprediction; zero cost when the guess is right.
\texttt{pc\_tgt = idex\_pc4 - 4 + idex\_imm} --- careful: pc+4
carried forward, not pc itself.
\end{example}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{figures/ch07/fig_flush}
\caption[Branch flush]{Predict-not-taken: free on the guess,
two slots on the miss.}
\label{fig:flush07}
\end{figure}

\begin{example}[a flush, with and without the squash]\label{ex:flush07}
Program \texttt{ch07\_br.S}: two \texttt{li}, a \texttt{beq} that is
taken, then two instructions \texttt{li a2,99} and \texttt{li a3,99}
that the branch jumps over, then \texttt{li a5,7}. By the time the
\texttt{beq} is decided in EX, the first wrong-path \texttt{li} is in
ID and the second is being fetched. Correct core: \texttt{a2}, \texttt{a3}
stay 0 and $F=4+2\cdot1=6$:
\begin{lstlisting}
CORE=fwd-pipe F=6 stalls=0 taken=1 lastret=-1
x10=00000001
x11=00000001
x15=00000007
\end{lstlisting}
The mutant that still redirects the pc but forgets to turn the two
wrong-path instructions into bubbles:
\begin{lstlisting}
CORE=fwd-pipe F=6 stalls=0 taken=1 lastret=-1
x10=00000001
x11=00000001
x12=00000063
x13=00000063
x15=00000007
\end{lstlisting}
\texttt{0x63}$=99$ in both: the instructions that the program says never
run, ran. The cycle count is identical, so only a state comparison
can find this bug --- timing alone would have called the mutant
correct.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[the scoreboard, all measured]\label{ex:score07}
Put the three costs into one formula and test it. If a program executes
$I$ instructions, hits $S$ load-use stalls and takes $K$ branches, the
core fetches the parking instruction after
\[
F = I + S + 2K
\]
cycles. For the sum-1..10 program of ch05: $I=37$, $S=1$ (the
\texttt{lw}/\texttt{addi} pair), $K=9$ (the \texttt{bne} is taken nine
times and falls through once): $37+1+18=56$. Measured (Linux x86\_64
container, Icarus 12):
\begin{lstlisting}
CORE=fwd-pipe F=56 stalls=1 taken=9 lastret=-1
x5=0000000b
x6=00000037
x7=0000000b
x10=00000037
x11=00000038
m0=00000037
m1=00000038
\end{lstlisting}
The registers and memory equal the single-cycle core's and the
reference model's. Compare ch05: the same program took $F=37$ there,
one cycle per instruction, but on a clock about five times slower. The
formula was also checked on a second program (the same loop with bound
6: $22+1+8=31$, measured $F=31$). The CPI of this run is
$56/37\approx1.51$ cycles per instruction, of which 18 of the 19 lost
cycles are branch flushes.
\end{example}

\section{The debugging receipts}
\label{sec:receipts07}
\index{X-propagation}

\begin{example}[two real bugs this chapter hit]\label{ex:bugs07}
Building this core produced two genuinely instructive failures,
kept because they are the lesson. (1) \emph{X-propagation deadlock}:
uninitialised pipeline registers made \texttt{stall} unknown
forever --- nothing ever retired; the fix was zeroing the tag
registers (\texttt{idex\_rd}, \texttt{exmem\_rd}, \texttt{memwb\_rd})
on reset, since 0 never matches a live register. (2) The
hand-assembled constant \texttt{0x00002613} was written down as
``\texttt{lw a2,0(zero)}'' --- it actually decodes as
\texttt{addi a2,zero,0} (opcode \texttt{0x13}, not \texttt{0x03}).
The ``load-use'' test never tested a load. The book's own assembler
now generates every test byte; hand hex is exactly the
decode-a-field-wrong mistake ch03 warned about. Two more were found
while re-running everything for this edition: (3) the branch-direction
flag \texttt{idex\_f30} was never assigned on the normal path, so a
branch would have acted on an unknown value; the earlier testbench
contained no branch at all, so this had never been exercised. It now
takes the low bit of \texttt{funct3}, and the sum program is the
regression test. (4) A combinational path from the data-memory output
into the forwarding mux made the load-use stall unnecessary in
simulation (see the ``take the stall away'' example) and was removed.
\end{example}

\section{Common mistakes}
\label{sec:mistakes07}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Forwarding to the wrong stage.} A store's data must
      forward into \texttt{dmem\_wdata}, not just the ALU --- easy to
      miss because loads exercise only the read path.
\item \textbf{No x0 guard.} \texttt{idex\_rd==0} must never trigger
      a forward --- register 0 has no writer to forward from.
\item \textbf{Older-wins mux order.} MEM/WB checked before EX/MEM
      gives the stale writer priority --- silent wrongness.
\item \textbf{Stalling without freezing pc.} Freezing IF/ID while
      pc advances re-fetches and duplicates the instruction ---
      we measured exactly that duplicate.
\item \textbf{Hand-assembling test vectors.} One wrong field and
      the test tests nothing (our \texttt{0x00002613}).
\item \textbf{Trusting a test that cannot fail.} Break the hardware
      on purpose (drop the stall, swap the mux order, skip the squash);
      if the test still passes it is not testing that feature.
\item \textbf{Forgetting uninitialised tags are x.} \texttt{x==x}
      evaluates x, poisoning every comparison --- reset the tags.
\end{itemize}

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:exercises07}

\begin{exercise}[easy]\label{ex:e07a}
Why does forwarding check EX/MEM before MEM/WB?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}[hint]
Two matching writers: the newer one's value must win.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[easy]\label{ex:e07b}
State the load-use stall condition in one sentence of English.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}[hint]
EX-stage insn is a load AND ID-stage insn reads its rd.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[medium]\label{ex:m07a}
\texttt{add a0,a1,a2; add a3,a0,a4; add a5,a3,a6}: how many
forwards, and from which stage each?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}[hint]
Two: a0 via EX/MEM, a3 via EX/MEM one cycle later (chain).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[medium]\label{ex:m07b}
Why does the x0 guard matter for forwarding correctness?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}[hint]
x0 is never written; a match would forward a meaningless value to
a consumer reading the constant.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[medium]\label{ex:m07c}
A taken branch costs 2 slots here. What pipeline change halves it
--- and what does it cost in hardware?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}[hint]
Resolve branches in ID: an early comparator + target adder in ID
stage; one slot lost instead of two.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[medium]\label{ex:m07d}
The tb counts 1 stall for our program. Write a program that stalls
exactly twice.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}[hint]
Two back-to-back load-use pairs, e.g. lw a0,0(x); addi a1,a0,1;
lw a2,4(x); addi a3,a2,1.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[hard]\label{ex:h07a}
Our hazard unit compares only rs1/rs2 of the ID instruction. Which
instruction classes need no second-operand check, and is
over-stalling for them harmful?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}[hint]
I-type/lw only read rs1 --- checking rs2 can false-stall on
instructions whose rs2 field is part of the immediate. Harm:
a wasted bubble, not wrongness.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[hard]\label{ex:h07b}
Design the branch-in-ID resolution: which registers must compare
early, what forwarding must extend into ID, and what new hazard
does it create?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}[hint]
rs1/rs2 compared in ID needs forwarding from EX/MEM and MEM/WB into
ID --- and a load producing the compare operand needs TWO stalls.
\end{solution}


\begin{exercise}[easy]\label{ex:e07c}
Fill in the cost, in lost cycles, of each situation on this core:
ALU result used by the next instruction; load result used by the next
instruction; load result used two instructions later; a taken branch.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}[hint]
0; 1; 0 (the forward from MEM/WB covers it; run as
\texttt{ch07\_gap.S}, \texttt{stalls=0}); 2.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[easy]\label{ex:e07d}
Use $F=I+S+2K$ for \texttt{ch07\_br.S} ($I=4$ executed instructions,
one taken branch, no load). What is $F$?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}[hint]
$4+0+2\cdot1=6$, matching the measured \texttt{F=6}.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[medium]\label{ex:m07e}
Predict $F$ for the sum program if the loop bound is 6
(\texttt{li t2, 6}; the loop runs for $i=1\dots5$). Count $I$, $S$, $K$
first.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}[hint]
$I=3+5\cdot3+4=22$, $S=1$, $K=4$ (taken four times, falls through on
the fifth). $F=22+1+8=31$. The test script runs exactly this variant
and measures \texttt{F=31 stalls=1 taken=4}.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[medium]\label{ex:m07f}
The sum program's CPI on this core is $F/I$ (ignoring the 4-cycle
drain). Compute it, and say what fraction of the lost cycles belongs
to branches.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}[hint]
$56/37\approx1.51$. Lost cycles $=56-37=19$, of which $2\cdot9=18$ are
branch flushes: $18/19\approx95\%$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[medium]\label{ex:m07g}
Insert one independent instruction between the \texttt{lw} and its
user in each pair of \texttt{ch07\_stall2.S}. How many stalls remain,
and what is the total instruction count?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}[hint]
None: a load-use pair at distance 2 needs no bubble (the data are
forwarded from MEM/WB). The program of 7 instructions becomes 9.
Moving independent work into the gap (``scheduling'') is exactly what
an optimising compiler does.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[hard]\label{ex:h07c}
In the mutant without the stall the sum program gives \texttt{a1}=1
instead of 56. Explain the value 1 from what the EX/MEM register holds
for a load.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}[hint]
For \texttt{lw a0,0(zero)} the EX stage computed the address
$0+0=0$ and that is what EX/MEM keeps; with no stall the \texttt{addi}
takes that 0 from the forward and adds 1.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[hard]\label{ex:h07d}
The program \texttt{ch07\_false10.S} (\texttt{lw a0,0(zero);
addi a1,zero,10}) is independent, yet the core stalls once
(\texttt{stalls=1}); changing 10 to 11 removes the stall
(\texttt{stalls=0}). Explain, and say how to fix the hazard unit.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}[hint]
The immediate 10 occupies instruction bits 24:20, the field the hazard
unit reads as \texttt{rs2}; 10 equals the load's destination
\texttt{a0}=x10, so it looks like a match. Fix: compare \texttt{rs2}
only for instructions that really read it (R-type, stores, branches),
using a ``reads rs2'' control bit from the decoder. The cost of the
bug is one wasted cycle, never a wrong result.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[hard]\label{ex:h07e}
Model (not run): suppose hardware predicted \emph{backward branches
taken} with no penalty when right, and still paid 2 cycles when wrong.
What would $F$ be for the sum-1..10 program, and what extra hardware
does ``no penalty when right'' secretly require?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}[hint]
All nine backward \texttt{bne}s are taken and
correctly predicted; only the final fall-through is a misprediction:
$F=37+1+2\cdot1=40$ instead of 56. ``No penalty'' requires knowing the
branch target in the same cycle the branch is fetched: a small table
(a branch target buffer) or decoding in IF; this is the cost of the
model's optimism.
\end{solution}
