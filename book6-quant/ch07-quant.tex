\chapter{Data engineering for market data}\label{ch:data}

\noindent Every conclusion in a quant book stands on the dataset under it. This chapter is the
un-glamorous half of the work: how market data arrives, how to clean it without lying to
yourself, and how to store it so that every number you later print can be reproduced byte-for-
byte. The dataset of record here is a real one: 1006 BTC/USD trade prints pulled from
Kraken's public Trades endpoint and pinned to \texttt{data/btc\_trades.csv}.

\section{What market data looks like}\label{sec:marketdata}

Market data arrives at three granularities. \emph{Trades} are the ground truth---one row per
executed print with timestamp, price, volume, taker side. \emph{Candles/OHLCV} are
aggregations of trades into fixed buckets---and are therefore only as honest as the bucketing
code. \emph{Book snapshots} capture resting liquidity at an instant. A research dataset is
usually stored at trade or 1-minute resolution, with every coarser view derived on demand.

\begin{example}\label{ex:clean}
\textbf{The first pass.} \texttt{data\_clean.py} runs the raw snapshot through dedup,
ordering, and a $|r|>8\sigma$ bad-tick screen:
\begin{lstlisting}[numbers=none]
raw 1006 prints -> dedup 990  (dupes 16)
price span 83731.0 .. 83915.3
return sd 0.27bp; |r|>8sd ticks: 4
max time gap between prints 7s  mean 0.6s
\end{lstlisting}
\begin{solution}
Sixteen exact duplicates (same $t$, price, volume) are feed artifacts---a venue emits the
same print on reconnect; dedupe or your volume double-counts. The 4 outlier ticks are not
automatically bad---at 0.27bp sd in a quiet tape, a legitimately jumpy print is $|r|>8\sigma$
without being an error; the screen \emph{flags for review}, it does not delete. That is the
cardinal rule: cleaning decisions get logged, never silently applied.
\end{solution}
\end{example}

\section{Resampling is a contract}\label{sec:resample}

An OHLCV bar is a summary \emph{contract}: o = first print of the bucket, h = max, l = min,
c = last, v = sum of volumes, n = print count. Any deviation---e.g.\ opening with the first
\emph{ask} instead of the first trade---breaks downstream consumers silently.

\begin{example}\label{ex:resample}
\textbf{Verify the contract.} \texttt{resample\_demo.py} builds 5-minute bars and asserts the
semantics on a real bucket:
\begin{lstlisting}[numbers=none]
1006 trades -> 3 5m bars
t=1791738600 o=83757.4 h=83757.4 l=83731.0 c=83746.5 v=0.462 n=129
t=1791738900 o=83746.5 h=83880.0 l=83746.5 c=83876.3 v=12.736 n=635
t=1791739200 o=83876.3 h=83915.3 l=83872.9 c=83906.7 v=2.134 n=242
bar semantics verified: o=first, h=max, l=min, c=last
\end{lstlisting}
\begin{solution}
The middle bar carries 635 prints and 12.7 BTC of volume---a real trade burst, not a
resampling artifact. The point is the \texttt{assert}: write the bar contract as executable
checks, because a swapped $h$/$l$ or a mean-instead-of-last close passes every eyeball test
and ruins every return downstream.
\end{solution}
\end{example}

\section{Gaps, clocks and time zones}\label{sec:gaps}

Missing data is information. \texttt{gaps\_detect.py} deletes a 3-minute window and finds it
by timestamp arithmetic:
\begin{lstlisting}[numbers=none]
expected 11 1m slots, have 8, missing 3
outage window: 1791739020 .. 1791739140 (3 bars)
rule: forward-fill l>c? never fill price; mark bar as gap and exclude from returns
\end{lstlisting}
The right response to a gap is to \emph{mark it}: a bar that never existed is not a flat
bar---forward-filling price manufactures fake zero-volatility data that looks calm and is
false. Return series should skip gap boundaries; volume sums should report them.

\begin{example}\label{ex:gapfill}
\textbf{The cost of filling.} Suppose a feed drops 5 minutes and you forward-fill the last
close. What happens to realized vol, ACF of returns, and a vol-targeted position size?
\begin{solution}
You inject five fake zero returns: the vol estimate dips (denominator up, no variance added),
$|\mathrm{ACF}(1)|$ moves toward zero diluting real clustering, and the vol-target sizes up
into a volatility vacuum that did not exist. Every downstream statistic is corrupted by a
fabrication that leaves no trace---which is why the rule is ``mark, don't fill.''
\end{solution}
\end{example}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\input{figures/ch07-gap}
\caption{A 3-minute outage is a hole, not a zero---never fabricate bars.}
\label{fig:gap}
\end{figure}

Crypto trades around the clock, equities have sessions, and every venue stamps time in its
own convention (seconds vs milliseconds vs nanoseconds, UTC vs exchange-local). One clock
rule fixes all of it: \emph{store everything in UTC epoch seconds; convert for display only.}
Mixing epochs in one column is the silent killer of joined datasets.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering

\caption{Sessions differ; timestamps must not. Store UTC epoch; display in whatever zone the
reader wants.}

\end{figure}

\section{Bad ticks and outliers}\label{sec:outliers}

A bad tick is a print inconsistent with the market around it: a stale quote, a test trade, a
data-feed glitch. The screen is simple---flag $|r_t|$ beyond a robust threshold (e.g.\
$8\times$ the sd of neighbouring returns)---but the disposition is judgment: crypto has real
wicks that look exactly like feed errors. Compare across venues when possible; a ``bad tick''
that prints on three venues is a real trade.

\begin{example}\label{ex:outlier}
\textbf{The 2-sigma trap.} A cleaner drops every $|r|>3\sigma$ tick on a fat-tailed tape.
What is wrong?
\begin{solution}
Under a normal model $3\sigma$ is $\sim$0.3\% of mass; under crypto's actual kurtosis
(ch1 measured excess $\approx+4.6$) the same cut discards a meaningful fraction of real
returns---you clean away the fat tails you are supposed to be modelling. Outlier screens need
a fat-tail-aware threshold and a review log, not a Gaussian reflex.
\end{solution}
\end{example}

\section{Pinning and storage}\label{sec:pin}

The methodology of this book is snapshot-first: fetch once, pin the raw bytes, record the
hash, and build every chapter number from the pinned file. \texttt{pin\_data.py}:
\begin{lstlisting}[numbers=none]
sha256 3450c146b18639b3...  bytes 44023
rule: every quoted number in the chapter reproduces from the pinned file offline
\end{lstlisting}
The practice scales: store raw fetches immutable; store derived frames separately (CSV for
eyeballs, Parquet for size); record fetch time, endpoint, parameters and hash in a sidecar.
If a number cannot be regenerated from a pinned file, it is an anecdote.

\begin{example}\label{ex:pin}
\textbf{The reproducibility audit.} A teammate reports ``BTC mean 1m return was $+2.1$bp on
2026-10-09.'' Write the minimal audit trail that makes the claim checkable offline.
\begin{solution}
The pinned trade file (or candle file), the code that computes the return, the time window,
and the input hash. Our own pin is \texttt{data/btc\_trades.csv} sha256
\texttt{3450c146\ldots}: anyone can regenerate every number in this chapter without network
access---which is the whole point.
\end{solution}
\end{example}

\begin{checkpoint}[Dataset hygiene]
\begin{itemize}
\item Which is ground truth: venue candles or trade prints? (Prints---candles are a derived
view; ch3 showed they can disagree at bucket edges.)
\item Your resampler emits a bar for every slot including empty ones. Is that a gap bug?
(Depends---mark empty slots explicitly; absence is not a zero-price bar.)
\item Name the two columns where a unit-mismatch (s vs ms) silently corrupts a join.
(Timestamp and anything keyed on it: every derived column.)
\end{itemize}
\end{checkpoint}

\section{Recap}\label{sec:datarecap}

Fetch raw, pin bytes, dedupe, screen ticks for review, mark gaps, enforce the OHLCV contract
by assertion, store UTC epoch. None of this is optional polish: it is the difference between
a backtest that means something and a sophisticated hallucination.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\small
\caption{Chapter toolbox.}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Concept & Rule & Gotcha\\
\midrule

Resample & o/h/l/c/v/n contract & assert on real buckets\\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\begin{example}[A 10-$\sigma$ move that is not a bad tick]\label{ex:sigmaflag}
\textbf{Strategy.} Run the chapter's $\sigma$-threshold rule
verbatim on the real print file and see how many flags it raises
at $2\sigma$, $5\sigma$, $10\sigma$.

\textbf{Solution.} \texttt{sigma\_flags.py} prints:
\begin{verbatim}
print-to-print moves: n=1005  std=0.22bp  max=2.34bp
|dp|>2sigma: 57 prints flagged
|dp|>3sigma: 16 prints flagged
|dp|>5sigma: 9 prints flagged
|dp|>10sigma: 1 prints flagged
largest move = 10.7 sigma but only 2.34bp -- not a bad tick
\end{verbatim}
The largest print-to-print move is $10.7\sigma$ yet only
$2.34$bp --- a normal price step at print granularity. The rule
``flag $>5\sigma$'' here quarantines nine good prints per 1006.

\textbf{Significance.} $\sigma$-thresholds are scale-fragile:
$10\sigma$ is enormous on daily bars ($\sim$30bp) and nothing on
prints. A bad-tick screen must be denominated in \emph{price
terms} (bp or ticks) with the sampling rate attached, then
$\sigma$ can assist --- never lead.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The arrival process is clustered]\label{ex:cluster}
\textbf{Strategy.} Measure the raw inter-print gaps on the pinned
tape before modelling anything: a Poisson clock would give an
exponential tail; real trade arrivals cluster.

\textbf{Solution.} \texttt{tz\_gap.py} prints:
\begin{verbatim}
inter-print gaps: n=1005  median 1ms
p95 2444ms  max 6.6s
gaps >20x median: 483 (48.1%)
prints/10s: mean 17.6  max 75  zeros 0
arrival process is clustered -- Poisson would show smoother counts
\end{verbatim}
Half the prints arrive within a millisecond of the last, yet the
tape still holds $6.6$s holes --- bursts of $75$ prints per 10s
alternating with silence. The distribution is neither Poisson nor
uniform: it is \emph{bursty}.

\textbf{Significance.} This is why the chapter insists resampling
is a contract: any statistic computed per-print inherits the
arrival process. A mean gap of 1ms does not mean the book updates
every millisecond --- it means the busy half does and the quiet
half does not. Sample at clock times, never at print times, for
anything a model will consume.
\end{example}

\section{Common mistakes}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Forward-filling prices across gaps.} Manufactures fake flat data and corrupts
  every downstream statistic.
\item \textbf{Trusting venue candles at bucket edges.} Rebuild candles from prints and
  compare---the two disagree at boundaries often enough to matter.
\item \textbf{Deleting outliers on a Gaussian threshold.} Fat tails are the object of study,
  not noise.
\item \textbf{Mixing epochs.} A seconds column and a milliseconds column join beautifully
  and corrupt silently---store one unit.
\item \textbf{Timezone-forgetting.} ``Exchange time'' is not a time zone; store UTC epoch,
  localise at display.
\item \textbf{Recomputing instead of pinning.} Data you fetched live yesterday is
  unreproducible today; pin raw bytes before touching them.
\item \textbf{$\sigma$-flags without a unit.} A $10\sigma$
print-to-print move is $2.3$bp --- real, not bad data
(Example~\ref{ex:sigmaflag}). Bad-tick screens need a price
denomination first.
\item \textbf{Cleaning before pinning.} Any filter applied before
the raw file is frozen destroys the evidence for why a value was
dropped --- pin first, clean on a copy, log every decision.\end{itemize}

\section{Exercises}
\begin{exercise}[easy]
What three fields does a raw trade print carry that a candle loses?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Precise timestamp, taker side (buy/sell aggression), per-print volume---the candle keeps only
bucket aggregates.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[easy]
Give the OHLCV contract for a bar of prints
$(p_1,\dots,p_n;v_1,\dots,v_n)$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
$o=p_1$, $h=\max_i p_i$, $l=\min_i p_i$, $c=p_n$, $v=\sum v_i$, $n$ = count.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[easy]
A feed emits each print twice on reconnect. Which cleaning step removes the artifacts, and
what exactly is a ``duplicate''?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Dedupe on exact $(t,\text{price},\text{volume})$ match---re-emitted prints are identical in
all three fields.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[easy]
Why is storing ``Exchange-local time'' a defect even when the venue never changes zones?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Local times collide across venues, DST-free conventions differ, and joins to other feeds
silently misalign---one epoch column is the only portable convention.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[easy]
Expected number of $|r|>3\sigma$ ticks in 1000 normal returns? In a fat-tailed tape?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
$\approx3$ under normal; far more under excess kurtosis---a fat-tail-naive cut discards real
data.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
From \texttt{data/btc\_trades.csv}: count prints per second, find the fastest second, and
state what burst-detection rule it suggests.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Group by integer second; the peak second's count over the median is the burst ratio---your
script's numbers are the answer.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
Design the gap-handling rule for a 30-day daily series: which steps get the gap bar, which
get nothing.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Returns: skip the boundary pair (no $r$ across a gap); volume: report missing; level plots:
break the line, never interpolate through the hole.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
Two venues' 1m candles disagree on open by 4c with identical closes. What kind of bucket-edge
bug produces that, and how do you detect it from prints?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
One venue labels the bucket by open-time, the other by close-time (or uses a different first-
print inclusion rule); rebuild candles from both venues' prints and compare labels.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
Write the sidecar metadata (JSON) for a pinned trades file.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
\verb|{"file","sha256","bytes","fetched_at","endpoint","params","rows"}|---enough to
reproduce the fetch and verify integrity.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
Why does a dedupe pass need to run \emph{before} the bad-tick screen?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Duplicates inject fake zero-return pairs that shrink $\hat\sigma$, making the screen
hyper-sensitive to real prints.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
Show that a 3-minute forward-fill in a 1440-day daily series biases realized vol by
$\approx-\sqrt{3/1440}$ fraction---order $4.6\%$ low.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Adding 3 zero returns among 1440 changes variance by $-3/1443$ roughly; the sd bias is half
that in logs---the point: even tiny fabrications are measurable.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
A colleague stores millisecond timestamps in a column named \texttt{ts} next to a seconds
column named \texttt{time}. What single convention prevents the resulting join bug?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
One column, one epoch, one unit---\texttt{ts\_utc\_s} everywhere; convert at read.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[hard]
Prove that deduping on $(t,p,v)$ can still leak volume: a venue may emit the same print with
two trade IDs. What is the robust dedupe key?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Include the venue trade ID when present; without it, dedupe on $(t,p,v,\text{side})$---the
exact-(t,p,v) rule can over-dedupe two genuinely simultaneous prints, which is the opposite
leak; the correct key is the venue's own id when available.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[hard]
Extend \texttt{resample\_demo.py} to emit empty-bucket markers for slots with zero prints;
verify your 1m series distinguishes ``gap'' from ``zero-volume minute''.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Iterate expected slots; absent slots get \texttt{n=0} markers vs missing-feed markers---
report the count of each in the snapshot.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[hard]
A bad-tick screen uses $|r|>8\sigma_{\text{neighbours}}$ where $\sigma$ is computed including
the suspect tick. Why does the suspect tick camouflage itself, and what is the fix?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
The tick inflates the very sd used to flag it---the denominator eats the signal. Exclude the
candidate from the sd estimate (leave-one-out), or use a robust scale (median absolute
deviation).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[easy]
A print-to-print move of $2.34$bp was flagged at $10.7\sigma$.
Explain in one line why the flag is wrong.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
$\sigma$ is computed on the same micro-scale distribution ---
$10\sigma$ of $0.22$bp is $2.3$bp, a normal step; the threshold
ignored the unit.
\end{solution}
\begin{exercise}[medium]
Write a bad-tick rule for this print file that flags $0$ of the
1006 real prints yet still catches a 50bp glitch.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
E.g.\ flag $|dp| > 20$bp \emph{or} price deviating $>0.1\%$ from a
5-print rolling median --- denominated in price terms, not
$\sigma$ of the print distribution.
\end{solution}
\begin{exercise}[hard]
Your feed timestamps prints in local time and the venue in UTC.
Design the dedup rule that survives a daylight-saving change, and
say what breaks if you dedupe on (price, local-time) instead.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Dedupe on (price, volume, UTC-ms) and re-anchor any local
timestamp to UTC before joining --- otherwise the DST hour-shift
duplicates every print in the overlap window.
\end{solution}

\section*{Notes}
The unglamorous 80\% of quant work is this chapter. Every ``alpha'' that ever collapsed in
production collapsed here first: a double-counted print, a forward-filled gap, a timezone
join. The discipline that survives contact with real feeds is simple---pin raw bytes, mark
everything, and make the bar contract executable.
