\chapter{The workshop: twenty contest problems}\label{ch:workshop}

Part mechanics, part method: twenty original problems that deliberately
mix chapters --- energy plus rolling, Faraday plus momentum. Each comes
with a hint (one line, for the stuck) and a full solution in the
\emph{Strategy}$\to$\emph{Solution}$\to$\emph{Significance} frame the
rest of the book uses.

\section{Contest method in one page}\label{sec:ws-method}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Units first}: any answer failing dimensional analysis is
wrong before it is checked.
\item \textbf{Limits before algebra}: $m\to0$, $\mu\to0$, $r\to\infty$
--- every formula you write should survive its limits.
\item \textbf{Conservation before integration}: check $p$, $L$, $E$
conservation \emph{first}; integrate only when forced.
\item \textbf{Audit, then trust}: every solution ends with a number you
can sanity-check against an order of magnitude.
\end{itemize}

\section{The problems}\label{sec:ws-problems}

\begin{description}
\item[W1] (medium; F=ma-style) A ball is thrown at $20$\,m/s; its
terminal speed is $33$\,m/s. What fraction of its weight is the drag at
launch? When does drag actually matter?
\item[W2] (medium; F=ma) A $3$\,kg block on a table ($\mu_s=0.4$,
$\mu_k=0.3$) ties over a pulley to a hanging mass. Find the largest
hanging mass that never moves, and the acceleration when it hangs
$2$\,kg instead.
\item[W3] (medium; F=ma) Ball A rolls at $2$\,m/s and hits identical
ball B elastically; B leaves a $1.25$\,m table and lands $1$\,m out.
Are the numbers consistent with a clean hit?
\item[W4] (medium; AP-C) A $1$\,kg ball at $2$\,m/s hits a stationary
$2$\,kg ball with $e=0.5$. Find both final speeds and audit the lost
energy.
\item[W5] (hard; APhO-style) A spool of outer radius $R$, inner $r$
rests on a table; a thread wound on the inner radius is pulled at
angle $\theta$ above horizontal. Which way does it roll?
\item[W6] (medium; F=ma data) A meter stick pivots about its $20$\,cm
mark. Find its small-oscillation period.
\item[W7] (medium; F=ma) A satellite in a circular $1.5R_E$ orbit
transfers to $2.5R_E$. Find the two burns and total $\Delta v$.
\item[W8] (hard; USAPhO) A $100$\,kg satellite docks with a spinning
$1000$\,kg station ($I=5000$\,kg\,m$^{2}$) at its $5$\,m rim; the
station spun at $0.5$\,rad/s. Find the new rate.
\item[W9] (hard; USAPhO classic) A frictionless tunnel runs straight
through a uniform-density Earth. Show the transit time to the far side
and explain why it does not depend on where the tunnel runs.
\item[W10] (hard; APhO) A $50$\,kg cart rolls at $5$\,m/s while sand
rains in vertically at $2$\,kg/s. Find $v(t)$ and explain the missing
force.
\item[W11] (medium; AP-C FR) Find the field on the axis of a uniformly
charged disk at height $z$; take the $z\gg R$ limit.
\item[W12] (hard; USAPhO) A point charge sits off-centre inside a
conducting shell's cavity. Find the charges induced on each surface
and argue the exterior field's shape.
\item[W13] (medium; F=ma) Two $10$\,g balls each with charge
$+1$\,\textmu C are released $10$\,cm apart. Speed when far apart?
\item[W14] (hard; APhO) A dielectric slab slides part-way into a
charged, \emph{isolated} capacitor. Find the work done by the field
pulling it in, and where the energy went.
\item[W15] (medium; F=ma data) A $10$\,k$\Omega$ voltmeter measures
across the $5$\,k$\Omega$ lower leg of a $10\text{k}\!+\!5$\,k divider
on $15$\,V. What does it read?
\item[W16] (hard; USAPhO) Charging a capacitor through a resistor
always dissipates half the battery's energy --- independent of $R$.
Where did it go? Resolve the ``paradox'' for $R\to0$.
\item[W17] (medium; AP-C FR) Design a velocity selector passing
$3\times10^{5}$\,m/s ions through a $0.01$\,T field.
\item[W18] (hard; USAPhO classic) A rod slides down vertical
frictionless rails joined by resistor $R$ in field $B$. Find its
terminal velocity and audit the powers.
\item[W19] (medium; AP-C) An RLC circuit rings at the same frequency as
a familiar mechanical oscillator. State both frequencies.
\item[W20] (hard; estimation) Deliver $1$\,kW to a $12$\,V load over
$0.5\,\Omega$ of line. Compare raw versus stepped-up:
estimate the loss reduction.
\item[W21] (medium; AP-C) An $8$\,g bullet strikes a $2.0$\,kg wood
block hanging on a $1.5$\,m string; the pair swings up $6.0$\,cm.
Find the bullet's speed and the kinetic-energy fraction that survived
the collision.
\item[W22] (medium; F=ma) A solid ball rolls without slipping across a
table at $2.0$\,m/s and off the $1.25$\,m edge. Where does it land
--- and does its spin change the answer versus a sliding puck?
\item[W23] (hard; USAPhO) A uniform non-conducting sphere carries
volume charge $\rho$. An electron released from rest at radius
$r_0<R$ oscillates. Show the motion is simple harmonic and compute the
period for $\rho=1$\,\textmu C/m$^{3}$.
\item[W24] (medium; AP-C) A $100$\,\textmu F capacitor charged to
$12$\,V discharges through a resistor into $1$\,mL of water.
Estimate the temperature rise, and explain why engineers don't heat
water with capacitors.
\item[W25] (hard; APhO-style) Two parallel wires $1$\,m apart carry
equal current $I$; the force per metre on each is exactly
$2\times10^{-7}$\,N/m. Find $I$ --- and name the definition this
encodes.
\item[W26] (medium; F=ma) A perfectly reflecting solar sail at $1$\,AU
has mass per area $1$\,g/m$^{2}$. Compare its radiation-pressure
acceleration with the Sun's gravity there: does it fall inward or
drift outward? (Take $S=1360$\,W/m$^{2}$.)
\item[W27] (hard; USAPhO) A rod of mass $0.10$\,kg and length
$0.40$\,m slides on horizontal frictionless rails joined by a
resistor, in a vertical field $B=0.50$\,T, launched at $2.0$\,m/s.
How much charge passes through the resistor before the rod stops
--- and why is the answer independent of $R$?
\item[W28] (hard; USAPhO) An Atwood pair ($m_1=2$, $m_2=1$\,kg) hangs
over a pulley of mass $M=1$\,kg, radius $R=0.10$\,m. Find the speed
after $m_1$ falls $0.50$\,m two ways --- dynamics, then energy ---
and audit that they agree.
\item[W29] (medium; F=ma) A rail launcher: a $50$\,g armature on
$2$\,m rails carrying $100$\,A across a $0.5$\,T field, $30$\,cm
wide. Find the exit speed --- and the assumption that makes this
optimistic.
\item[W30] (medium; AP-C) An electron at $10^{7}$\,m/s crosses $5$\,cm
deflection plates $1$\,cm apart at $100$\,V. Find the deflection and
exit angle --- the oscilloscope's working principle.
\item[W31] (medium; F=ma) A pendulum clock rides an elevator
accelerating upward at $g/2$. By what factor does its period change
--- and what does the same math say in free fall?
\item[W32] (medium; estimation) A $10$\,g lead bullet at $500$\,m/s
stops in a block. Could its kinetic energy melt the bullet? ($c_{\rm
Pb}=128$\,J/kg\,K, $L_{\rm Pb}=24$\,kJ/kg, mp $=327^{\circ}$\,C.)
\item[W33] (medium; estimation) A $100$\,W bulb puts $5\%$ into visible
light at $550$\,nm. How many photons per second --- and how many hit
your eye's $5$\,mm pupil at $10$\,m?
\item[W34] (hard; APhO-style) Ions of charge $e$ accelerated through
$2000$\,V enter a $0.2$\,T mass-spectrometer field and curve at
$r=15$\,cm. Find their mass in atomic units --- the machine's whole
job.
\item[W35] (medium; F=ma) An EV carries $60$\,kWh at $400$\,V, draws
$150$\,A cruising, and consumes $0.25$\,kWh/km. Estimate power,
range, and what a $20\%$ battery bump buys.
\item[W36] (hard; USAPhO) A satellite at $1$\,AU needs $1$\,kW of bus
power from $30\%$-efficient cells ($S=1360$\,W/m$^{2}$). Size the
array, then discuss what eclipse and pointing cost on top.
\item[W37] (medium; AP-C) An ideal transformer feeds a $12$\,V,
$5$\,A load from a $120$\,V line. Find the turns ratio, the primary
current, and the power audit that confirms ideal.
\item[W38] (medium; F=ma) A $10$\,mH inductor is switched across a
$1$\,\textmu F capacitor holding $1$\,mC. Find the ringing frequency
and the peak current --- and name the chapters this problem lives
between.
\item[W39] (hard; USAPhO) A cyclotron with $B=1.2$\,T, radius
$0.40$\,m extracts protons at the rim. Find their kinetic energy in
MeV --- the machine's spec in one line.
\item[W40] (medium; AP-C) Geosynchronous orbit: find the radius and
altitude for $T=24$\,h over Earth ($M=6.0\times10^{24}$\,kg,
$R_E=6.4\times10^{6}$\,m).
\item[W41] (hard; USAPhO) W18's rod, now on a $30^{\circ}$ incline:
$m=0.10$\,kg, $R=1\,\Omega$, $B=0.50$\,T, $L=0.40$\,m. Find the
terminal speed --- and check the power balance.
\item[W42] (medium; AP-C) A battery reads $12.4$\,V open-circuit and
$11.9$\,V delivering $2$\,A. Find its EMF and internal resistance,
and estimate the short-circuit current.
\item[W43] (hard; USAPhO) A bar magnet drops through a horizontal
conducting ring. Describe the ring's current direction on approach,
at the midpoint, and on exit --- and identify what supplies the
energy the ring dissipates.
\item[W44] (medium; F=ma) A diesel cylinder compresses air
adiabatically $20{:}1$ by volume from $300$\,K. Find the final
temperature ($\gamma=1.4$) --- and why no spark plug is needed.
\item[W45] (medium; AP-C) A $20$\,mH inductor carrying $3$\,A is
suddenly shorted through a $10\,\Omega$ resistor. Find the initial
decay time constant and the total heat the resistor sees.
\item[W46] (hard; APhO-style) A point charge sits $d$ above a
conducting plane. Use the image method to find the force attracting
it --- and say what real object this models.
\end{description}

\section{Hints}\label{sec:ws-hints}

\begin{description}
\item[W1] Drag $\propto v^{2}$; at terminal it equals $mg$.
\item[W2] Static friction supplies up to $\mu_sN$ --- check the
boundary first.
\item[W3] Equal-mass elastic = swap; then free-fall from the table.
\item[W4] $v_1'=v_1(1-e)/\text{...}$ --- use the restitution formulas;
loss $\propto(1-e^{2})$.
\item[W5] Torque about the contact point: $F(R\cos\theta-r)$.
\item[W6] $T=2\pi\sqrt{I/(mgd)}$ with $d$ the pivot--CM distance.
\item[W7] Hohmann: vis-viva at both burns.
\item[W8] Conserve $L$ about the spin axis; $L_{new}=I_{tot}\omega'$.
\item[W9] $g(r)\propto r$ inside a uniform sphere --- that's Hooke's
law.
\item[W10] Sand carries no horizontal momentum: $dP=0$ along the track.
\item[W11] Symmetry leaves only the $z$-component to integrate.
\item[W12] Conductor field zero $\Rightarrow$ inner surface $=-q$;
conservation fixes the outer.
\item[W13] Energy: stored $\to$ kinetic of \emph{both}.
\item[W14] Isolated means $Q$ fixed: $U=Q^{2}/2C$ drops as $C$ grows.
\item[W15] The meter is a parallel resistor: re-compute the divider.
\item[W16] The dissipation is in the resistor \emph{or} the spark ---
either way it isn't in the cap.
\item[W17] $qE=qvB$ when undeflected.
\item[W18] At terminal: gravity power $=$ dissipated power.
\item[W19] $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$ is chapter~7's $\sqrt{k/m}$ with the
map already made in chapter~14.
\item[W20] $I^{2}R$ scales the wrong way at low $V$: step up first.
\item[W21] Momentum through the collision; energy through the swing.
\item[W22] The flight is pure projectile; what survives of the spin?
\item[W23] Gauss gives $E\propto r$ inside --- that is a spring.
\item[W24] All the stored energy ends as heat, whatever $R$ is.
\item[W25] $F/L=\mu_0I^{2}/(2\pi d)$.
\item[W26] Pressure $=2S/c$ for a reflector; compare with
$GM_\odot/r^{2}$.
\item[W27] Impulse per step $BIL\,dt=BL\,dq$; the total sees only
$\Delta p$.
\item[W28] $a=(m_1-m_2)g/(m_1+m_2+I/R^{2})$; or spend
$(m_1-m_2)gh$ on translation \emph{and} rotation.
\item[W29] $F=BIL$ over the rail length; energy form is fastest.
\item[W30] Field time $=L/v_0$; deflection is a $\tfrac12at^{2}$
projectile sideways.
\item[W31] Effective $g$ changes; $T\propto g^{-1/2}$.
\item[W32] KE first, then warm + melt terms separately.
\item[W33] Energy per photon $hc/\lambda$; pupil's share of the
sphere's area.
\item[W34] Eliminate $v$: $m=qB^{2}r^{2}/(2V)$.
\item[W35] $P=VI$; range $=$ battery $/$ per-km use.
\item[W36] Divide by $\eta S$; eclipse needs stored night power too.
\item[W37] $V_2/V_1=N_2/N_1$; amps invert it. Power both sides.
\item[W38] $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$; peak current from
$\tfrac12Q^{2}/C=\tfrac12LI^{2}$.
\item[W39] Centripetal: $r=mv/qB$; out pops $KE=(qBR)^{2}/2m$.
\item[W40] Kepler: $r^{3}=GMT^{2}/4\pi^{2}$.
\item[W41] Terminal: $mg\sin\theta=B^{2}L^{2}v/R$; audit
$mgv\sin\theta$ vs $I^{2}R$.
\item[W42] $V_{\rm term}=\mathcal E-Ir$; two readings, two unknowns.
\item[W43] Lenz on entry and on exit are opposite; gravity pays.
\item[W44] Adiabat: $TV^{\gamma-1}$ is constant; compare $T$ to
fuel autoignition.
\item[W45] $\tau=L/R$; all stored energy ends as heat.
\item[W46] Image charge $-q$ at depth $d$; force between the pair.
\end{description}

\section{Solutions}\label{sec:ws-sol}

\begin{example}[W1: drag bound]\label{ex:w1}
At launch the ball moves at $20$\,m/s with $v_t=33$\,m/s.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Quadratic drag: $F_D=mg(v/v_t)^{2}$.
\begin{solution}
$F_D/mg=(20/33)^{2}=0.37$: drag is already $37\%$ of weight at launch
--- it matters everywhere for a baseball-speed throw; only
$v\ll v_t/3$ lets you ignore it. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} The ``when does drag matter'' question has a
number: the ratio $(v/v_t)^{2}$, not vibes.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W2: pulley + friction boundary]\label{ex:w2}
$3$\,kg on the table, $\mu_s=0.4$, $\mu_k=0.3$.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Static boundary: $m_2g=\mu_sm_1g$.
\begin{solution}
Boundary at $m_2=1.2$\,kg: nothing moves for $m_2\le1.2$\,kg.
With $m_2=2$\,kg sliding:
$a=(2-0.9)g/5=2.16$\,m/s$^{2}$. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Static/kinetic regimes have different answers;
always find the boundary first.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W3: collision + projectile consistency]\label{ex:w3}
Does a $2$\,m/s approach give a $1$\,m landing at $1.25$\,m height?
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Elastic equal masses swap velocities; then
projectile time.
\begin{solution}
B takes $2$\,m/s. Fall time $t=\sqrt{2(1.25)/9.8}=0.505$\,s;
range $=2\times0.505=1.01$\,m --- consistent to rounding. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Two chapters in one problem; the chain is the
point.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W4: restitution chain audit]\label{ex:w4}
$m_1=1$, $m_2=2$, $v_1=2$, $e=0.5$.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Restitution formulas + energy before/after.
\begin{solution}
$v_1'=\frac{1-0.5\cdot2-2}{3}(2)/1$... cleaner:
$v_1'=(m_1-em_2)/(m_1+m_2)\,v_1=0$,
$v_2'=(m_1(1+e))/(m_1+m_2)\,v_1=1$\,m/s.
$K_i=2$\,J, $K_f=1$\,J: loss $=1$\,J $=(1-e^{2})\,\frac12\mu v^{2}$
with $\mu=2/3$: $(0.75)(0.5)(2/3)(4)=1.0$\,J. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} The energy loss is entirely fixed by $e$ ---
nothing else to compute.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W5: the spool's direction flip]\label{ex:w5}
Thread on inner radius $r$ pulled at $\theta$.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Torque about the instant contact point.
\begin{solution}
$\tau=F(R\cos\theta-r)$: rolls \emph{toward} the pull when
$\cos\theta>r/R$ (small angle), away when steep. At
$\theta=\arccos(r/R)$ it slides without rolling. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} The unintuitive answer falls out of one torque
line --- the classic reason to pick the contact point as pivot.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W6: meter-stick period]\label{ex:w6}
Pivot at the $20$\,cm mark; CM distance $d=0.3$\,m.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Physical pendulum $T=2\pi\sqrt{I/mgd}$.
\begin{solution}
$I=I_{cm}+md^{2}=\tfrac1{12}+0.09=0.173$\,kg\,m$^{2}$;
$T=2\pi\sqrt{0.173/2.94}=1.53$\,s --- the code confirms $1.526$\,s.
\checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} A one-centimeter pivot error is a percent-level
period error --- the measurement design \emph{is} the physics.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W7: Hohmann $1.5\to2.5R_E$]\label{ex:w7}
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Visc-viva at perigee and apogee.
\begin{solution}
$v_c=6458$\,m/s; burns $762$ and $670$\,m/s; total
$\Delta v=1432$\,m/s. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Two tangential burns beat any continuous burn
for circular-to-circular transfer.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W8: satellite capture]\label{ex:w8}
$100$\,kg at the $5$\,m rim of a $5000$\,kg\,m$^{2}$ station at
$0.5$\,rad/s.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} $L$ conserved: $\omega'=L/(I+md^{2})$.
\begin{solution}
$L=5000(0.5)=2500$; $I'=5000+2500=7500$;
$\omega'=0.33$\,rad/s. The ``lost'' $L$ went into the cart's CM ---
which orbits the docking point. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Angular momentum is never lost; it just moves
into translation you forgot to count.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W9: tunnel through Earth]\label{ex:w9}
Uniform density: find the transit time to the far side.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Inside a uniform sphere $g(r)=gr/R$ ---
a Hooke's-law restoring force.
\begin{solution}
$\ddot x=-(g/R)x$: SHM with $T=2\pi\sqrt{R/g}=84.4$\,min; the far
side lands at $T/2=42.2$\,min --- \emph{independent} of which chord
the tunnel takes (same restoring constant). \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} 42 minutes to anywhere --- the famous number is
the uniform-density answer, and the sim confirms it.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W10: sand in the cart]\label{ex:w10}
Sand falls \emph{vertically} at $2$\,kg/s into a $5$\,m/s cart.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} $dp_x/dt=0$ along the track (the sand adds no
horizontal momentum).
\begin{solution}
$Mv=\mathrm{const}$: $v(t)=Mv_0/(M+\dot mt)=250/(50+2t)$.
The cart slows because its mass grows --- no horizontal force needed.
\checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} ``Missing force'' problems are bookkeeping
problems: the momentum left the cart through the added mass.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W11: disk axis field]\label{ex:w11}
Charge $\sigma$ on a disk of radius $R$; find $E$ at height $z$.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Only the $z$-component survives; integrate rings.
\begin{solution}
$dE_z=k\,dq\,z/(r^{2}+z^{2})^{3/2}$;
$E(z)=\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(1-\frac{z}{\sqrt{z^{2}+R^{2}}}\right)$.
For $z\gg R$: $E\to kQ/z^{2}$ (point limit). \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Every integration exercise ends with a limit
that should reproduce a familiar answer --- check it.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W12: induced charges in a shell]\label{ex:w12}
Charge $+q$ sits off-centre inside a neutral shell's cavity.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Field inside conductor $=0$ forces a surface
accounting.
\begin{solution}
Inner surface: total $-q$ (non-uniform --- it crowds near the charge).
Outer: $+q$ spread \emph{uniformly} if the shell is spherical ---
the exterior field is that of a centered $+q$ regardless of the
cavity's mess. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} The conductor screens details: outside sees
only the total.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W13: two released charges]\label{ex:w13}
$+1$\,\textmu C each, $10$\,g each, released $10$\,cm apart.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Energy into \emph{both} kinetic energies.
\begin{solution}
$U_i=kq^{2}/r=0.09$\,J; each gets $mv^{2}/2=0.045$:
$v=3.0$\,m/s each (relative $6.0$). \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Split the energy by mass before computing
speeds --- a common one-body mistake.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W14: dielectric pull-in]\label{ex:w14}
Slab slides into an \emph{isolated} capacitor at fixed $Q$.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} $U=Q^{2}/2C$ falls as $C$ grows: the drop is the
field's mechanical work.
\begin{solution}
With fraction $x$ of area filled: $C(x)=(\varepsilon_0A/d)(1+(\kappa-1)x)$;
$W_{\rm field}=U_i-U_f$ is positive --- the field \emph{pulls the slab
in}; the lost energy becomes the slab's kinetic energy (or a hand's
heat). \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Same setup, battery attached instead: the sign
flips --- fixed $V$ means the battery pays. Know which reservoir
you're holding constant.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W15: the lying voltmeter]\label{ex:w15}
$10$\,k$\Omega$ meter across the $5$\,k leg of a
$10\text{k}+5\text{k}$ divider on $15$\,V.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} The meter joins the lower leg in parallel.
\begin{solution}
$R_{leg}=5\|10=3.33$\,k; reading
$=15\times3.33/13.33=3.75$\,V vs the ideal $5$\,V --- the meter lies
by $25\%$. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Every measurement perturbs the circuit; the
only question is by how much.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W16: the RC half-and-half ``paradox'']\label{ex:w16}
Battery $\mathcal E$ charges $C$ through $R$: half the energy always
burns.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Compute both sides; then ask what $R\to0$ changes.
\begin{solution}
Battery: $\mathcal EI$ integrated $=\mathcal EQ=\mathcal E^{2}C$.
Stored: $\tfrac12C\mathcal E^{2}$. Burned: the other half, always ---
the code prints $0.100=0.050+0.050$. For $R\to0$ the current rings up
with stray inductance and the loss rides out in the ringing (or the
spark): the ``paradox'' dissolves into \emph{where} the half goes,
not \emph{whether} it goes. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Conservation never has paradoxes; only missing
channels.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W18: falling rod terminal speed]\label{ex:w18}
Rod $m=0.05$\,kg, $L=0.4$\,m, $R=2\,\Omega$, $B=0.5$\,T on vertical
rails.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} At terminal: $mg=ILB$, or equivalently
$mgv=\mathcal E^{2}/R$.
\begin{solution}
$v_t=mgR/(B^{2}L^{2})=24.5$\,m/s; audit: gravity power

\end{solution}

\begin{example}[W19: the same maths twice]\label{ex:w19}
$L=10$\,mH, $C=1$\,\textmu F.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$.
\begin{solution}
$\omega_0=10^{4}$\,rad/s, $f=1592$\,Hz --- identical to a
$k=100$\,N/m, $m=1$\,kg oscillator; the map is
$m\leftrightarrow L$, $k\leftrightarrow1/C$. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Once you've solved the oscillator you've solved
every oscillator --- the circuit is a rebadged spring.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W20: the design estimate]\label{ex:w20}
$1$\,kW to a $12$\,V load over $0.5\,\Omega$ of line.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} $I^{2}R$ at each voltage.
\begin{solution}
At $12$\,V: $I=83$\,A, loss $=I^{2}R=3.5$\,kW --- the line eats more
than the load gets. Step up to $120$\,V: $I=8.3$\,A, loss
$=35$\,W --- a $100\times$ cut from a $10\times$ boost.
\checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Chapter~18's whole story in one estimate:
voltage is the loss-killer.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W21: the ballistic pendulum]\label{ex:w21}
$8$\,g bullet, $2.0$\,kg block, swing height $h=6.0$\,cm.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Two stages, two laws: momentum for the hit, energy
for the swing.
\begin{solution}
Swing: $v'=\sqrt{2gh}=1.08$\,m/s. Hit:
$v_{\rm bullet}=(M+m)/m\cdot v'=251\times1.08=272$\,m/s.
Energy kept: $K_f/K_i=m/(M+m)=1/251\approx0.4\%$ --- nearly all of
it left as heat and deformation. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} The collision stage forbids energy conservation;
the swing stage forbids momentum bookkeeping. Mixing the stages is
the classic error --- the two-law chain is the lesson.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W22: rolling off the table]\label{ex:w22}
Solid sphere at $v=2.0$\,m/s off a $1.25$\,m table.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Flight is chapter~1 projectile; the spin only
changes the energy audit.
\begin{solution}
$t=\sqrt{2h/g}=0.505$\,s; range $=vt=1.01$\,m --- identical to a
sliding puck's. The spin persists in flight (no torque about the
CM), so the ball lands still rotating with the same $\omega$; its
total in-flight $K=\tfrac{7}{10}mv^{2}$ simply rode along.
\checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Rolling changes the energy account, not the
kinematics: $v$ is $v$ no matter how the mass was spinning.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W23: an electron's private spring]\label{ex:w23}
Uniform $\rho$ sphere, electron released at $r_0<R$.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Gauss inside a uniform sphere gives $E\propto r$
--- a linear restoring force is a spring.
\begin{solution}
$E(r)=\rho r/3\varepsilon_0$, so $F=-eE=-(\rho e/3\varepsilon_0)r$:
SHM with $\omega=\sqrt{\rho e/3\varepsilon_0m}
=8.1\times10^{7}$\,rad/s, $T=77$\,ns at $\rho=1$\,\textmu
C/m$^{3}$.
Any chord, any $r_0<R$: same period --- W9's gravity tunnel with
charge instead of mass. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Inverse-square laws degrade to Hooke's law
inside uniform bodies; learn to spot the spring in disguise.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W24: the capacitor is a terrible kettle]\label{ex:w24}
$100$\,\textmu F at $12$\,V into $1$\,mL of water.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Every joule stored ends as heat, independent of
$R$.
\begin{solution}
$U=\tfrac12CV^{2}=\tfrac12(10^{-4})(144)=7.2$\,mJ;
$\Delta T=U/(mc)=7.2\times10^{-3}/4.19=1.7$\,mK.
Capacitors deliver \emph{power}, not energy: a battery of the same
size carries $10^{4}\times$ more joules. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Chapter~12 computed \emph{how} the energy gets
out; this is the audit of \emph{how much} there was --- energy
density is the engineering number.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W25: the ampere as a force]\label{ex:w25}
Two wires $1$\,m apart, force per metre $2\times10^{-7}$\,N/m.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} $F/L=\mu_0I^{2}/(2\pi d)$; solve for $I$.
\begin{solution}
$I=\sqrt{2\pi d(F/L)/\mu_0}
=\sqrt{2\pi\times1\times2\times10^{-7}/4\pi\times10^{-7}}=1$\,A.
This \emph{is} the pre-2019 SI definition of the ampere --- a force
balance between wires, abandoned when fixing $e$ became the better
anchor. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Units come from measurable setups: the ampere
was an experiment you could bolt to a bench before it became a
constant.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W26: the sail that beats gravity]\label{ex:w26}
Reflecting sail, $\sigma_m=1$\,g/m$^{2}$, at $1$\,AU.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Radiation pressure on a reflector is $2S/c$;
compare the acceleration with $GM_\odot/r^{2}$.
\begin{solution}
$F/A=2S/c=9.1$\,\textmu N/m$^{2}$; $a_{\rm sail}=9.1$\,mm/s$^{2}$
vs $g_\odot=GM_\odot/r^{2}=5.9$\,mm/s$^{2}$ --- the sail wins and
drifts \emph{outward}. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Light pushes hard enough to unbind a
light-enough craft --- the LightSail premise, and a reminder that
$1/c$ is small but not zero.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W27: charge through the rails]\label{ex:w27}
Rod $m=0.10$\,kg, $L=0.40$\,m, $B=0.50$\,T, $v_0=2.0$\,m/s.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Each step's impulse is $BIL\,dt=BL\,dq$; sum to
stopping.
\begin{solution}
$\sum BL\,dq=BLQ=\Delta p=mv_0$, so
$Q=mv_0/(BL)=0.2/0.2=1.0$\,C --- whatever $R$ sets for the
\emph{schedule} of the slowdown. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Like the RC energy audit: the totals are
kinematic, only the rates are Ohmic. $R$ decides \emph{when}, never
\emph{how much}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W28: the massive pulley's share]\label{ex:w28}
$m_1=2$, $m_2=1$\,kg; pulley $M=1$\,kg, $R=0.10$\,m; drop
$h=0.50$\,m.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Dynamics: $a=(m_1-m_2)g/(m_1+m_2+I/R^{2})$.
Energy: spend the lost potential on both kinds of $K$.
\begin{solution}
$a=9.8/(3+0.5)=2.8$\,m/s$^{2}$; $v=\sqrt{2ah}=1.67$\,m/s.
Energy: $\Delta PE=4.9$\,J;
$K=\tfrac12(m_1+m_2)v^{2}+\tfrac12I\omega^{2}
=4.2+\tfrac14Mv^{2}=4.2+0.7=4.9$\,J --- audited to the joule.
\checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} The pulley's $0.7$\,J is exactly what the
massless-pulley answer misses; two methods agreeing is what
``understood'' feels like.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W29: the rail launcher's optimism]\label{ex:w29}
$F=BIL=0.5\cdot100\cdot0.3=15$\,N on $0.05$\,kg over $2$\,m.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Work $F\ell$ becomes $\tfrac12mv^{2}$.
\begin{solution}
$W=30$\,J $\Rightarrow v=\sqrt{2W/m}=\sqrt{1200}=35$\,m/s.
The optimism: current holds $100$\,A only while a stiff supply feeds
the growing back-EMF $BLv$ (about $5$\,V by exit); rail resistance,
friction and inductance all shave the number. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} The same back-EMF that limits a motor
(chapter~18) limits the launcher --- one formula, two industries.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W30: steering a beam]\label{ex:w30}
Electron $v_0=10^{7}$\,m/s, plates $L=5$\,cm, $d=1$\,cm, $V=100$\,V.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Axial motion untouched; transverse is
$\tfrac12(eE/m)t^{2}$ with $t=L/v_0$.
\begin{solution}
$E=10^{4}$\,V/m; $a=eE/m=1.76\times10^{15}$\,m/s$^{2}$;
$t=5\times10^{-9}$\,s; deflection $y=2.2$\,cm;
exit angle $\tan\theta=v_y/v_0=at/v_0=0.88$, $\theta=41^{\circ}$.
\checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Chapter~1's projectile with $qE/m$ in place of
$g$: the CRT oscilloscope was literally this two-line problem sold as
an instrument.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W31: time dilation for elevators]\label{ex:w31}
Pendulum in an elevator accelerating up at $a=g/2$.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} The accelerating frame adds a uniform fictitious
force --- replace $g$ by $g_{\rm eff}=g+a$.
\begin{solution}
$T'=2\pi\sqrt{L/(g+a)}=T\sqrt{g/(1.5g)}=0.816T$ --- the clock runs
$22\%$ fast. In free fall $g_{\rm eff}=0$: no restoring force, no
period, the clock is just debris. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Einstein's elevator made homework: `weight'
is a frame-dependent reading, which is exactly what an accelerometer
measures.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W32: the bullet's own funeral]\label{ex:w32}
$10$\,g of lead at $500$\,m/s, initially at $20^{\circ}$\,C.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Compare KE with the two heat costs: warming to the
melting point, then the latent step.
\begin{solution}
$KE=1250$\,J. Warming: $mc\Delta T=0.01\cdot128\cdot307=393$\,J.
Melting: $mL=240$\,J. Total $633$\,J $<1250$\,J --- it melts, with
$617$\,J to spare for warming the puddle or the block's share.
\checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Chapter~4's energy audit in one paragraph:
separate the budget lines before spending the joules.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W33: photons are cheap, eyes are small]\label{ex:w33}
$100$\,W bulb, $5\%$ at $550$\,nm, pupil $D=5$\,mm at $r=10$\,m.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Power into photons via $E_\gamma=hc/\lambda$; then
geometry's area ratio.
\begin{solution}
$E_\gamma=3.6\times10^{-19}$\,J, so
$\dot N=5/3.6\times10^{-19}=1.4\times10^{19}$/s emitted. The pupil
covers fraction $\pi(D/2)^{2}/4\pi r^{2}=D^{2}/16r^{2}
=1.6\times10^{-8}$ --- call it $2\times10^{11}$\,photons/s reaching
the eye. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Detection is abundant-photon statistics; the
faint-object problem is geometric, not energetic --- telescopes buy
$\times10^{4}$ by area, not brighter bulbs.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W34: weighing what you cannot see]\label{ex:w34}
$e$-charged ions, $V=2000$\,V, $B=0.2$\,T, $r=0.15$\,m.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Accelerator: $qV=\tfrac12mv^{2}$; spectrometer:
$r=mv/qB$. Eliminate $v$.
\begin{solution}
$m=qB^{2}r^{2}/(2V)=1.6\times10^{-19}(0.04)(0.0225)/4000
=3.6\times10^{-26}$\,kg $\approx22$\,u --- sodium-ion territory.
\checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Energy and momentum bookkeeping chained
chapters 4 and 13 into chemistry's mass scale --- isotope
identification is a radius measurement.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W35: the EV's two numbers]\label{ex:w35}
A $60$\,kWh pack feeds a $400$\,V bus; the car draws $150$\,A
cruising and uses $0.25$\,kWh per km.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Power is $VI$; range is a division of budgets.
\begin{solution}
$P=60$\,kW cruising ($81$\,hp); range $=60/0.25=240$\,km.
A $20\%$ bigger pack buys $48$\,km --- range scales exactly with
stored joules, which is why battery kWh \emph{is} the spec.
\checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Chapters 12 and 4 meet in one consumer
question; there is no cleverness left, only energy density.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W36: acreage for a kilowatt]\label{ex:w36}
$1$\,kW bus, $\eta=0.30$, $S=1360$\,W/m$^{2}$.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Incident $S$ times area times efficiency equals
bus power; then second-order the real orbit.
\begin{solution}
$A=P/(\eta S)=1000/408=2.45$\,m$^{2}$ of cells --- a door's worth.
Real arrays add margin: off-point cosine loss, degradation, and the
eclipse side needing batteries sized for the dark half of every
$90$-minute lap. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} W26's flux becomes an area budget: the same
$1.4$\,kW/m$^{2}$ pays for the whole spacecraft if you spend it
right.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W37: the transformer's bargain]\label{ex:w37}
$12$\,V $\times$ $5$\,A load on a $120$\,V line, ideal unit.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Turns set volts; ideal means power is conserved,
which sets amps.
\begin{solution}
$N_1/N_2=V_1/V_2=10{:}1$; secondary power $=60$\,W so the primary
draws $60$\,W too: $I_1=0.5$\,A. Voltage ten-fold down, current
ten-fold up --- the trade is exact. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} The $\times10$ both ways is why W20's step-up
works: a transformer's exchange rate is what bought the grid AC.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W38: where the energy sloshes]\label{ex:w38}
$L=10$\,mH, $C=1$\,\textmu F, initial $Q=1$\,mC.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} The LC pair is chapter~7's oscillator with
charge as position.
\begin{solution}
$\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}=10^{4}$\,rad/s, $f=1.6$\,kHz.
Energy sloshes capacitor$\leftrightarrow$inductor:
$\tfrac12Q^{2}/C=\tfrac12LI_{\max}^{2}$ gives
$I_{\max}=Q/\sqrt{LC}=10$\,A.
\checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} W19 named the map; this runs it with numbers
--- $10$\,A peak from a millicoulomb is the LC leverage radios tune
for.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W39: the cyclotron's price tag]\label{ex:w39}
$B=1.2$\,T, $R=0.40$\,m, protons extracted at the rim.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} The rim radius \emph{is} the momentum:
$p=qBR$; convert.
\begin{solution}
$KE=(eBR)^{2}/2m_p=(1.6\times10^{-19}\times1.2\times0.4)^{2}
/(2\times1.67\times10^{-27})=1.8\times10^{-12}$\,J
$\approx11$\,MeV.
\checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} The magnet's $B$ and the tank's radius are the
\emph{entire} energy budget --- bigger magnets or bigger machines,
there is no third knob (relativity aside, which is Book~7's
problem).
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W40: the shelf at 36,000 km]\label{ex:w40}
Geosynchronous period $T=86{,}400$\,s over Earth.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Kepler: $r^{3}=GMT^{2}/4\pi^{2}$; altitude is
$r-R_E$.
\begin{solution}
$r^{3}=6.67\times10^{-11}\cdot6.0\times10^{24}\cdot(8.64\times10^{4})^{2}
/39.5=7.6\times10^{22}$\,m$^{3}$; $r=4.2\times10^{7}$\,m,
$h=r-R_E\approx3.6\times10^{7}$\,m $\approx5.6R_E$.
\checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} There is exactly one geosynchronous radius ---
a real-estate shelf every satellite dish on Earth points at,
and the reason Clarke published before Sputnik.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W41: downhill terminal velocity]\label{ex:w41}
Rod $0.10$\,kg, incline $30^{\circ}$, $R=1\,\Omega$,
$B=0.50$\,T, $L=0.40$\,m.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Terminal means gravity's slope component equals
the braking force --- or just audit powers.
\begin{solution}
$v=mgR\sin\theta/(B^{2}L^{2})=0.49/(0.04)=12.2$\,m/s.
Audit: gravity feeds $mgv\sin\theta=6.0$\,W; the resistor burns
$I^{2}R=(BLv/R)^{2}R=6.1$\,W --- matching to the cent.
\checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} W18 asked for the audit; the incline makes the
driving term $\sin\theta$ explicit. Energy conservation \emph{is}
the terminal condition.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W42: the battery's honest rating]\label{ex:w42}
$12.4$\,V open, $11.9$\,V at $2$\,A.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} $V_{\rm term}=\mathcal E-Ir$: open circuit reads
$\mathcal E$ directly; the loaded reading prices $r$.
\begin{solution}
$\mathcal E=12.4$\,V; $r=(12.4-11.9)/2=0.25\,\Omega$.
Short-circuit $I=\mathcal E/r\approx50$\,A --- a quarter-ohm is why
the same battery sags under starter load but floats fine on the
multimeter. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Example~\ref{ex:maxpower} with real numbers:
the internal resistance you can't see is what decides a source's
usefulness --- measure it by loading, not by reading.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W43: gravity powers the brake]\label{ex:w43}
Bar magnet falling through a conducting ring.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Lenz at each stage: the ring opposes the
\emph{change} in flux, not the flux itself.
\begin{solution}
Approach: upward flux grows $\to$ ring's field points down, current
as it repels the magnet. Midpoint: flux at maximum, change
$\approx0$, current $\approx0$. Exit: upward flux falls $\to$ ring's
field points up, current reverses to attract the magnet back.
The dissipated $I^{2}R$ heat is paid by the magnet's lost kinetic
energy --- the fall is slowed, and gravity is the power supply.
\checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Eddy-current braking's whole mechanism in one
drop: oppose the change, conservation pays the bill.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W44: compression is the spark]\label{ex:w44}
Air at $300$\,K, $20{:}1$ adiabatic compression, $\gamma=1.4$.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Adiabat in $TV$ form: $T_2/T_1=(V_1/V_2)^{\gamma-1}$.
\begin{solution}
$T_2=300\times20^{0.4}=300\times3.31\approx993$\,K --- well past
diesel autoignition ($\sim600$\,K). The piston's compression work
became internal energy; ignite by temperature, not by spark.
\checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Chapters 4 and 17 fused: work $to$ internal
energy without heat exchange is the engine's entire thermodynamic
trick.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W45: the inductor's last stand]\label{ex:w45}
$L=20$\,mH, $I_0=3$\,A, discharged through $R=10\,\Omega$.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} Freewheel decay: $i(t)=I_0e^{-t/\tau}$,
$\tau=L/R$; energy stored goes entirely to heat.
\begin{solution}
$\tau=2$\,ms; $U=\tfrac12LI_0^{2}=90$\,mJ all lands in the resistor.
No battery, no capacitor --- the field itself pays out every joule.
\checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} The flyback example~\ref{ex:flyback} in its
safe form: give the current a path and the stored energy is a resistor
problem, not an arc.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[W46: the charge that sees its reflection]\label{ex:w46}
Point charge $q$ at height $d$ over a conducting plane.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Strategy.} The plane's boundary condition is reproduced by an
image $-q$ at depth $d$; the real force equals the pair's attraction.
\begin{solution}
$F=kq^{2}/(2d)^{2}=kq^{2}/4d^{2}$ toward the plane --- the charge is
attracted to its own image. This is why a charge near a wall or a PCB
ground plane pulls toward it, and why ESD events choose sharp edges
where the image sits closest. \checkmark
\end{solution}
\textbf{Significance.} Chapter~10's conductor boundary solved by
symmetry instead of integration --- the same trick that computes
capacitance to ground in every layout tool.
\end{example}

\section{Common mistakes}\label{sec:ws-mistakes}

\begin{tripwire}[Conservation skipped for integration]
Reaching for $\int F\,dx$ when $E$ is conserved --- always check the
conservation laws first.
\end{tripwire}
\begin{tripwire}[Limits left unchecked]
A formula that fails $\mu\to0$ or $r\to\infty$ is wrong; check the
limit before trusting the algebra.
\end{tripwire}
\begin{tripwire}[One-body answers for two-body problems]
Both charges move, both balls swing --- split the energy first.
\end{tripwire}
\begin{tripwire}[Units carried loosely]
One $v^{2}$ forgotten becomes a factor of $10^{4}$ error; carry units
like cargo.
\end{tripwire}
\begin{tripwire}[``Missing'' momentum mourned]
Momentum isn't lost; it moves into the channel you ignored (the sand,
the station's CM, the radiated half).
\end{tripwire}
